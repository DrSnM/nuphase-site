Selected technical work

Contamination screening from spectra

88–94% field-validated accuracy classifying petroleum contamination directly from infrared spectra, across three regulatory thresholds and 2,700+ independently lab-measured samples. Built for Ziltek's RemScan, running on real sites for Chevron and Kuwait Oil Company, and included in a CONCAWE-funded comparative evaluation of field TPH technologies.

Soil carbon at national scale

Modelled soil organic carbon from spectra across 15,000+ real, lab-referenced samples nationally (R²=0.85), externally validated on 350+ samples never seen in training. Taken through to real commercial deployment, including a soil-carbon monitoring program for Chevron and an ACCU Scheme-compliant sampling-strategy design.

Soil texture from a handheld scanner

Built a compositional-statistics model that treats sand/clay/silt as a single constrained composition, predicting soil texture directly from an infrared scan — developed in partnership with a commercial soil-testing laboratory: 93–94% texture-class accuracy in the field.

Physical and statistical modelling beyond a spreadsheet

Building the physics and validation engine behind Groundswell (in active development): real wave physics, satellite imagery, and computer vision, tested against real ocean buoy networks through a custom reliability gate built to stop an unreplicated result from being trusted.

Validation discipline as the actual deliverable

Caught a model that looked strong under casual cross-validation (R²=0.65) collapse to R²=-2.8 under a properly rigorous, leakage-safe check, then root-caused it to exactly how the physical soil samples were being repacked between scans. Reporting that a model doesn't work yet, and exactly why, is the same discipline that makes the ones that ship trustworthy.

Case study

Groundwork: from a question to a live product

I built Groundwork to make public wage and employer records useful when someone is weighing a job, a move or a layoff notice. I lead the product decisions and use Codex and Claude Code to turn them into scoped work, build in parallel where it makes sense, and review changes independently.

Early interest in the layoff pages informed the direction. It was a small sample of search traffic, enough to tell me where to look next and too small to say anything about demand.

Each tool reviews the other’s work

Claude on the first layoff article

Claude spotted that a line about Groundwork’s saved copy could sound like it contradicted a notice visible on the state website. The published article now states what was checked and what the result means.

Read the article ↗
Codex on the article-update tool

Claude built the first version. Codex caught two proposed updates that could try to overwrite the same working draft, and a final write failure that could leave changed inputs behind. That led to a recovery and concurrency review. It was caught during development, and gave me concrete failures to test before the workflow was put into use.

Three things I take into any project

  1. Define what done means. Write down the goal, what stays the same and how the result will be checked before anyone starts. A clear brief makes it easier to build and much easier to review.
  2. Ask what could break. Give the reviewer the change, the original requirements and the evidence, and ask for a case that would prove the change wrong.
  3. Keep the decisions with you. The tools help me see more options and check more cases. I decide what is worth building, what the evidence supports and when it is ready to ship.
